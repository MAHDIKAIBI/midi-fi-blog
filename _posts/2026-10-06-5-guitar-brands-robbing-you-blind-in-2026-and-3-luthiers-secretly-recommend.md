<p>Crack open the back of a nine hundred dollar guitar, and you will find maybe a hundred and fifty to two hundred dollars worth of wood, wire, and plastic staring right back at you. There is no rare tonewood and no secret pickup formula, just a logo stamped across the headstock that turns out to be the most expensive component bolted onto the instrument.</p><p>You feel that disconnect every time you swipe your card for a famous name brand. You are not just paying for pristine frets or a dialed in factory setup. It is built with the exact same neck pocket, the same slab body, and the same two single coil pickups you will see hanging on a showroom wall three states away. Step the component grade up, and that number climbs to about two hundred dollars. Build that identical spec sheet over in Indonesia, and you are still sitting right around two hundred dollars. Move that production run to Korea, and it jumps to four hundred dollars. Assemble that exact same build in the United States, and you are suddenly staring at one thousand to fourteen hundred dollars in manufacturing costs alone.</p><p>Here is the mechanism nobody puts on the hang tag. Core hardware does not automatically get better just because the stamp on the headstock changes countries. A CTS potentiometer sourced for a Korean build, and a CTS potentiometer ordered for a Chinese assembly line, can be the exact same part with the exact same tolerance rating. A Player Series body can easily clear seven hundred dollars retail purely on brand recognition, long before a single fret ever gets dressed.</p><p>And that fretwork is exactly where the lottery begins. Player reports are packed with stories of brand-new instruments arriving with sharp fret ends that shred your fingers during a bend. An automated Plek pass only analyzes what it is told to measure, meaning it will completely sail past a physical tooling mark, or a nut slot cut a hair off-center.</p><p>You are not gambling on baseline engineering. It is the exact same alder or ash body stock, sitting at forty-four to forty-five millimeters of thickness. You get the same maple or mahogany neck blank cut on the exact same jigs that feed the regular assembly line.</p><p>What you are actually buying is the marketing wrapper, paying for phrases like hand-finished, master-builder-signed, and small-batch production.
